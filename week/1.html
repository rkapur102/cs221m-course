  • Lecture 2 Review of language models

Each capability of language models was born out of the single training objective of repeatedly predicting the next token across an enormous amount of text. These models were never explicitly instructed that the Eiffel Tower is located in Paris, for example, or on how to ensure a pronoun agrees with its subject. Or, to take a more personal case, on what tone to use when communicating with someone who is distraught because they just lost their job. Those abilities are consequences of that one training objective; the procedures behind each ability were never written down and encoded in models by a person. Understanding and visualizing what those internal procedures are and ensuring they are aligned with intended reasoning and behavior is a new subfield of artificial intelligence called mechanistic interpretability (and it’s what our course is about!).

What would even count as a non-black-box explanation for a behavior of a language model? Take a model completing the prompt “The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of ___”. One answer might focus on what the model does. It completes prompts like this correctly most of the time, often performing better on famous landmarks rather than obscure ones, and it further improves as the model gets larger. That is an explanation at the level of behavior, where we're only concerned with input and output and never need to look into model internals. A second type of answer proposes a procedure: the model recognizes the subject of the sentence, retrieves an attribute it associates with that subject, and writes the attribute out. Now we have an algorithm with named steps and intermediate values, but notice how we still never make claims about where within the model those steps take place. A third type of answer changes this by pointing at the internal composition: the information leading to the resulting fact (“Paris”) is stored in the weights of a particular layer. We confirm this through a simple experiment: copying that layer’s “activations” from a run about a different world landmark makes the model answer with the wrong city.

All three of these count as valid and unique forms of explanation. Behavioral accounts carry over to both new tasks and larger models while avoiding making claims about the internals. Mechanistic accounts are exact about the internals and often hold only for the one model and task studied. The question you pose about language model behavior decides which type of explanation within interpretability is best suited to pursue. As such, the methods in this course are organized around questions, and the frameworks of thinking you will practice follows from them. Notice as well that the third (mechanistic) answer is supported by a test and potential solution. If the reason for a behavior really does trace back to where we claim within the internals, then intervening there should change the output in a specific and predictable way. Building experiments of that shape will take up most of the course to come!

The second half of this first week puts the above vocabulary in context, as we cannot make claims about language models without understanding what they are in much more depth first! A language model writes by producing a probability for every possible next token, sampling one, and then repeating. This means it can only ever condition on previous token outputs. Every token gets its own vector, and those vectors move through the layers side by side in the residual stream. Within a layer, attention is the only component that carries information between token positions, and the MLP (multi-layer perceptron) stores information relating to each position on its own. You will also use the Transformers library to load real open-source models and inspect their internals. Pay close attention there, as reaching into various running models and retrieving activations is something we will do repeatedly in what's to come in this course! Let’s get started with this review.
