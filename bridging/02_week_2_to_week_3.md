We started last week outside the model, examining the validity of benchmark scores. Do they truly back their claims? Consider whether models answer multiple-choice benchmark questions correctly because 1) they reasoned through the problems, or less desirably 2) because they encountered those exact questions during pre-training! Accuracy numbers on their own cannot separate the two possibilities. To see this, we built **minimal contrasting pairs**, taking questions the model already answered correctly, changing one aspect of format, and observing resulting behavior to diagnose. When we permuted the answer options (keeping every word of the question intact and only moving the correct answer to a different letter choice), we saw that accuracy still slipped. This suggests that models sometimes remember a multiple-choice letter rather than a correct answer! As a sharper test, we substituted the numbers in a question so that its correct answer had to be recomputed, but then placed the original answer back among the choices. On one geometry question, in which an ant crawls between two points, we saw that the model chose the original answer, 5 + 2√13, on all five versions of the question, even though the new coordinates made that value wrong each time.

Those two experiments exposed two very different model failure modes. Reacting to a change that should not matter is **oversensitivity**, and failing to react to one that should matter is **overstability**. In the notebooks, *both* failure modes appeared in the same model and in the same benchmark! This is worth remembering whenever single numbers or benchmark scores are offered as evidence of competence (they are often insufficient). Notice also how much care went into the perturbations themselves. Each variant we created held the wording, structure, and style of the distractors fixed so that exactly one aspect was modified. This is a necessary design choice to isolate a single exact cause of unintended behavior.

We spent the second half of the week going back inside the model, where we looked at three related probing methods. All of them use a linear transformation of the model’s internal state to decode information from it. The first, the **logit lens**, reuses the model’s own unembedding layer at every layer and every token, reading each intermediate activation as the final one. When we prompted the model in the notebook to translate “amor” from Spanish to French, the middle layers decoded to “love” just before the model produced “amour”, even though English was never explicitly requested in the translation process. Suspicious, yet informative! However, the logit lens can only show us information stored the same way as in the final layer, and many cells in our plot decoded to tokens with no clear connection to the prediction. Those cells might hold nothing useful, or they might hold information in a form the unembedding can’t read, and the logit lens cannot tell us which. **PCA** sidesteps that limit: without any labels, it finds the directions along which activations vary most. In the notebook, at layer 10 those directions alone split English-to-French prompts from English-to-Spanish prompts into two clusters. Finally, where PCA finds whatever variation happens to be largest, a supervised **linear probe** is trained to decode *exactly* the information we ask for. Given the layer-20 activations of short sentences such as “Hello.” and “Bonjour.” in four languages, the probe learned to tell which language each sentence was written in.

Then came the catch! An effective probe finds a direction that separates two groups, but such a direction may not always be the one the model actually *uses*. We saw this through the example of Prof. X, who wants to predict who will pass the final given data on how many assignments each student turned in along with their average grade. A supervised linear probe, which had looked like the most promising of our three methods, neatly separated the students who passed from those who failed. But its direction is chosen to draw the sharpest dividing line, and when we added it to a failing student’s two numbers to move them toward “pass,” it raised their grade and *lowered* the number of assignments they turned in, which is counterintuitive! The **difference-in-means** approach instead subtracts the average failing student from the average passing one, giving an arrow that follows how the groups actually differ: higher grades *and* more assignments. We then did the same inside the model, with the English and French sentences from the probing example: subtracting their average activations at layer 20 gave an arrow pointing from English toward French. Given the prompt “Bon appetit!”, the model normally continues in English, but adding that arrow to its activations as it generated text made it continue in French. A probe’s accuracy alone never tells us whether its direction matters in this way; that takes an intervention like this one.

And so establishing that same causal direction is what this next week is about. A question has been building since the course opened: is some internal component we have identified responsible for the behavior or output we associate it with? We will turn that question into an experiment (note the question-forward approach here!). Our intention is to intervene: change something inside the model on purpose, then see whether the output changes in the way our explanation predicts. **Ablations** remove information from a component and ask whether the behavior persists without it. **Interchange interventions** take an activation from one input and place it into the run of another, and ask whether the output now reflects the modified input that activation carried forth. These come with a formal vocabulary, built on causal mediation analysis and causal abstraction, for stating precisely when a step-by-step algorithm we can describe in words gives a faithful account of what the model computes internally. Simple correlations have carried us a long way. Now let's test them!
