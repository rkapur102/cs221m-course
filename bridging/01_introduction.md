Welcome to the course. The question we are here to answer is easy to ask and hard to settle: what is actually going on inside a modern neural network, and how would we ever know? These models are trained rather than designed. Nobody wrote down the rules they follow, and nobody can read those rules off the weights. So the only way to find out how a model works is to study it the way you would study any system you did not build — by running experiments on it.

That study is called **interpretability**, and over the next ten weeks we will build up its toolkit: reading hidden activations to see what information they carry, intervening on them to change what the model does, and testing whether a part of a network really plays the role we think it does. Our examples will mostly be language models, and our emphasis will be on **causal** evidence — not just noticing that something in the model correlates with a behavior, but showing that it causes it.

Before any of those tools make sense, though, the first lecture asks a question that comes one step earlier: what would even *count* as an explanation of a model's behavior? It turns out there is no single right answer. You can explain a model at the level of its **behavior** (what it does, and how that changes with training data or scale), at the level of the **algorithms** it seems to be running, or at the level of the **mechanisms** in its weights and activations that carry those algorithms out. Each level is a real explanation. Each buys you something different: finer detail at one end, broader prediction and control at the other.

That is the frame worth carrying into the rest of the quarter. Interpretability is not a fixed list of methods to memorize; it is a set of questions, where the question you are asking determines which method can answer it. The lecture works through all three levels with examples from current research, so you will see the whole map before we start walking through it piece by piece.
