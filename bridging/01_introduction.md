You are about to spend ten weeks taking language models apart. These systems write essays, pass exams, and hold conversations, and nobody can say with much confidence how they do it. The weights were learned from data, so the rules a model follows were never written down by anyone, and the only way to find out what those weights compute is to run experiments on the model itself. That work is called **interpretability**, and this course is a practical introduction to it.

Most of the quarter runs through notebooks you will execute yourself. You will read a model's hidden activations to see what information they carry (**probing**), push on those activations to change what the model says (**steering**), and run causal experiments that test whether a particular piece of the network really does the job you suspect it does. Language models are the running example throughout, and later in the quarter researchers from interpretability labs in academia and industry come in to talk about what they are working on.

Today's lecture opens with a question that shapes everything after it: what would it even mean to explain a model? The answer we will land on is that explanations arrive at several levels. There is the model's **behavior**, meaning what it does and how that shifts with scale and training data. There is the level of **algorithms**, the procedures it appears to be carrying out. And there are the **mechanisms**, the specific weights and activations that run those procedures. All three count as explanations, and each one costs you something: the finer your account of the internals gets, the narrower the range of things it lets you predict.

That map is worth carrying with you for the whole quarter. Every method we cover is an answer to some particular question, so the question you care about is what tells you which tool to reach for. Today you get to see all three levels at once, with examples from current research, before we start working through them one at a time.
