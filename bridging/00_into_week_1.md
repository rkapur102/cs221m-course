Each capability of a language model came out of a single training objective: to repeatedly predict the next token across an enormous amount of text. Nobody explicitly instructed the model that the Eiffel Tower is in Paris, or how to ensure a pronoun agrees with its subject, or what tone to use when communicating with someone who just lost their job. Those abilities are consequences of that one training objective, and the procedures behind each ability were never written down and encoded in models by a person. Working out what those internal procedures are, ensuring they are aligned with intended reasoning and behavior, and gathering evidence that our understanding of them is correct: that work is *interpretability*, the new field this course is about.

What would even count as a non-black-box explanation for a behavior of a language model? Take a model completing the prompt “The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of”. One type of answer describes what the model does: it completes prompts like this correctly most of the time, it does better on famous landmarks than obscure ones, and it improves as the model gets larger. That is an explanation at the level of **behavior**: we never had to look into model internals to give it. A second kind of answer proposes a procedure: the model recognizes the subject of the sentence, retrieves an attribute it associates with that subject, and writes the attribute out. Now we have an **algorithm**, with named steps and intermediate values. A third kind of answer points at the machinery: this fact (“Paris”) is stored in the weights of a particular layer, and we know this because copying that layer’s “activations” from a run about a different landmark makes the model answer with the wrong city.

All three of these count as valid *and* unique forms of explanation. Behavioral accounts carry over to both new tasks and larger models while avoiding making claims about the internals. Mechanistic accounts are exact about the internals and often hold only for the one model and the one task you studied. The question you pose about language model behavior decides which type of explanation within interpretability is worth crafting, so the methods in this course are organized around questions, with the toolkit following from them. Notice as well that the third answer comes with a test (and potential solution) attached. If the reason for a behavior really does trace back to where we claim, then intervening there should change the output in a specific and predictable way. Building experiments of that shape will take up most of the course to come!

The second half of this first week puts the above vocabulary in context, as we cannot make claims about language models without understanding what they are in much more depth first! A language model writes by producing a probability for every possible next token, sampling one, and repeating, which means it can only ever condition on what came before. Every token gets its own vector, and those vectors move through the layers side by side in the **residual stream**. Within a layer, **attention** is the only component that carries information between token positions, and the **MLP** (multi-layer perceptron) stores information relating to each position on its own. You will also use the Transformers library to load real open-source models and inspect their internals, as everything to come in the course assumes you can reach into a running model and retrieve an activation.
