Everything a language model can do came out of a single training objective: predict the next token, over and over, across an enormous amount of text. Nobody sat down and told it that the Eiffel Tower is in Paris, or how to keep a pronoun agreeing with its subject, or what tone to take with someone who just lost their job. Those abilities are consequences of that one objective, and the procedures behind them were never written down by a person. Working out what those procedures are, and gathering evidence that our account of them is right, is what this course is about.

We open by asking what would even count as an explanation, which sounds abstract until you try to answer it for something specific. Take a model completing the prompt "The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of". One kind of answer describes what the model does: it completes prompts like this correctly most of the time, it does better on famous landmarks than obscure ones, and it improves as the model gets larger. That is an explanation at the level of **behavior**, and we never had to open the model up to give it. A second kind of answer proposes a procedure: the model recognizes the subject of the sentence, retrieves an attribute it associates with that subject, and writes the attribute out. Now we have an **algorithm**, with named steps and intermediate values. A third kind of answer points at the machinery: this fact is stored in the weights of a particular layer, and we know because copying that layer's activations from a run about a different landmark makes the model answer with the wrong city.

The lecture's argument is that all three of these count as explanations, and each one gives you something the others cannot. Behavioral accounts carry over to new tasks and larger models while staying quiet about the internals. Mechanistic accounts are exact about the internals and often hold only for the one model and the one task you studied. The question you walk in with decides which level is worth working at, so the methods in this course are organized around questions and not around a fixed toolkit. Notice as well that the third answer came with a test attached. If the fact really is stored where we claim, then intervening there should change the output in a specific and predictable way. Building experiments of that shape takes up most of the quarter.

The second half of the week puts the vocabulary in place, since claims about machinery are empty without knowing what the machinery is. A language model writes by producing a probability for every possible next token, sampling one, and repeating, which means it can only ever condition on what came before. Every token gets its own vector, and those vectors move through the layers side by side in the **residual stream**. Within a layer, **attention** is the only component that carries information between token positions, and the **MLP** (multi-layer perceptron) works on each position on its own. You will also load real open-source models and inspect their insides, since everything later in the course assumes you can reach into a running model and pull out an activation.
