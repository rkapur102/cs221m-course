Give a language model the prompt `17 + 25 =` and it will answer 42. Nobody programmed it to carry the one. Its weights were fit to enormous amounts of text by a training process that only ever asked it to predict the next token, and whatever procedure those weights ended up carrying out was never written down by a person. Working out what that procedure is, and finding evidence that our account of it is right, is what this course is about.

Monday's lecture starts with a question that sounds abstract and turns out to be very practical: what would count as an explanation of that answer? Suppose we say the model is good at arithmetic, that it gets two-digit sums right most of the time, and that its accuracy climbs as the model gets larger. That is a real explanation, pitched at the level of **behavior**, and we never had to open the model to give it. Suppose instead we say the model computes the ones digit, checks whether that sum passed ten, and adds the carry into the tens column. Now we have proposed an **algorithm**, a procedure with named steps and intermediate values like the carry. Or suppose we go further and claim that the carry lives in a particular direction of a particular hidden vector at layer nine, so that editing that vector changes the tens digit and leaves the ones digit alone. That is a claim about **mechanisms**, about the specific machinery inside.

The lecture's argument is that all three count, and that each buys you something the others do not. Behavioral accounts travel well and let you predict what happens on new tasks or at larger scale, though they stay silent about the internals. Mechanistic accounts are precise about the internals and often hold only for the one model and one task you studied. So the question you actually care about is what tells you which level to work at, and the methods in this course are organized around questions instead of around a fixed toolkit. Something else worth noticing in that carry example: the mechanistic claim came with a test attached. If the carry really lives in that vector, then editing it should change the answer in a specific way. Most of the quarter is spent building experiments of exactly that shape.

Wednesday brings us back to the ground floor, because you cannot make claims about machinery without knowing what the machinery is. A language model writes by producing a probability for every possible next token, sampling one, and repeating, which means it can only ever look backward at what came before. Each token gets a vector, and those vectors travel through the layers side by side in the **residual stream**. Inside each layer, **attention** is the one component that moves information between token positions, and the **MLP** (multi-layer perceptron) works on each position by itself. You will also load real open-source models and inspect them, since every technique that comes later assumes you can reach into a running model and pull out an activation.
