Last week, we dissected a simple language model. At its core, given the tokens so far, a language model produces probabilities for every possible next token, samples from that distribution, and then repeats. In this way, it is **autoregressive**: it can only consider previous context and never future words. In the notebook, we saw this directly when changing a word *after* “Paris” left the model’s representation of “Paris” untouched. Even though it predicts one token at a time, the model processes all the previous tokens in parallel. Each token gets its own vector, and these vectors sit side by side in the **residual stream**, which runs through many layers. The vector at the last position after the last layer becomes the prediction.

Each layer has two parts, applied in sequential order: attention, then an MLP. **Attention** is how information moves between tokens; it captures word relationships, and it is the only part of the model that can do this. Every token’s vector is multiplied by a learned matrix to produce a query (“what information am I looking for?”) and by a different learned matrix to produce a key (“what information do I have?”). The dot product of one token’s query with another token’s key gives a relevance score, and tokens with high scores get their information pulled in (weighted by that score). In the notebook, we saw this as attention maps, including a “head” that copied patterns from earlier in the sequence.

The **MLP** (multi-layer perceptron) is a basic neural net that runs on each token separately: we multiply the (attention) vector by a weight matrix, apply a nonlinearity, multiply by another matrix, and add the result back into the residual stream. It gets no information from other tokens, so anything it needs from elsewhere in the sentence is brought in by attention first. MLPs make up roughly 70% of the model’s weights, and their activations are sparse, meaning only a handful of neurons fire strongly for any given token. That combination makes them a natural place for the model to store information. All of these weights, for attention and MLPs alike, were learned from one training signal: predicting the next token across huge amounts of training data (from conversation and text on the Internet!).

When the course opened, we sorted explanations into three levels: a model’s behavior, the algorithms it appears to be running, and the mechanisms that carry them out. This week sits at the first of those, which is a full explanation in its own right, and a good deal of what we know about these models lives there. Instead of going inside the model, we go back *outside*, considering whether it solves tasks the right way. A model can score well on a benchmark by using a shortcut or by having memorized test questions during training, but this sort of satisfactory performance does not always signal reasoning, understanding, and competence in unseen situations. To distinguish, we will build minimal pairs, which are two inputs that differ in exactly one way: where some changes should move the answer and some shouldn’t. We will also look at *input attribution*, which measures how much each input token contributed to a prediction.
